Body mechanics & mobility assessment

Muscles are the motor, bones are the levers, and nerves run the show.

How movement works

Skeletal muscle

Movement (main job), posture and positioning, and body heat.Example: shivering is muscles making heat.

Bones

Support, protect organs, produce blood cells in red marrow, store calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, iron, and lipids, and move with the muscles.Example: the skull protects the brain.

Connectors

Tendons: muscle to bone. Ligaments: bone to bone. Cartilage: lets bones glide and absorbs shock. Synovial joints: fluid-filled, allow bending.Example: the Achilles tendon connects the calf muscle to the heel bone.

Nerves

Spinal cord nerve cells fire signals that make muscles contract and pull on bone. CNS = brain + spinal cord; PNS = the nerves branching off it.Example: deciding to take a step sends signals down the spinal cord to the leg muscles.

Proprioception

Feedback between muscles and brain that tells you where your body is. Lets you balance and fine-tune movement. Also called kinesthesia.Example: touching your nose with your eyes closed.

Memory hook · tendons vs ligaments

Tendons Tie muscle To bone. Ligaments Link bone to bone.

The three principles of body mechanics

Lower back injuries are the most common problem for health care workers. Nurses have the highest rate of back injuries, usually from manual lifting and repositioning.

Alignment

Back straight, chin level, abs tight. Ear, shoulder, hip, knee, and ankle on one vertical line.

Balance

Feet shoulder-width apart, knees bent. A lower center of gravity and a wider base = more stable.

Movement

Keep the load close, face the client, pivot your feet instead of twisting.

DoDon't
Raise the bed to your working heightReach with extended arms
Bend your knees and lift with your legs (quadriceps)Bend at the waist or lift with your back

Ergonomics

Designing and arranging the work environment to fit the worker. Examples: height-adjustable beds, shower chairs, two-person lifts, transfer devices, side-opening linen bins, removing uneven floors. Solo lifts are the opposite.

Moving a client up in bed, in order

  1. Assess mobility
  2. Get lift assistance
  3. Lock the bed wheels
  4. Raise the bed
  5. Cross the client's arms over the chest
  6. Use the draw sheet
  7. Lower the bed to the lowest position

Activity intolerance

Loss of the ability to do tasks because of inactivity. Older adults are especially susceptible.

Watch for (during activity)

Weakness, fatigue, lightheadedness, chest pain, diaphoresis, significant vital sign changes.

Slides content HR, BP, and RR rise with activity and should return to baseline with rest.

Borg RPE scale

Client rates their own effort from 6 (resting) to 20 (max effort). Target: 12–14, somewhat hard to strong.

Mobility assessment

Done before first mobilizing the client, repeated per policy (every shift to every 24 hr), and after procedures that change mobility. Tells you the interventions, transfers, fall precautions, and devices the client needs.

Assess: usual mobility, ability to sit, stand, and walk, need for help, degree of mobility, skin condition, and manifestations during activity.

Quick check

A client can sit on the edge of the bed but can't hold it for 2 minutes. Level?

Level 1, maximum assist. Failing either Level 1 task keeps them there.

Which is correct body mechanics: bending slightly at the waist, or pivoting to move the client?

Pivoting. Bending at the waist and twisting both load the spine.

On the RPE scale, what range should activities fall in?

12–14. Somewhat hard to strong.

Complications of immobility

Bed rest for as little as 1 week can affect ADLs. Every body system takes a hit.

Musculoskeletal

Disuse osteoporosis

Bones thin and weaken without weight-bearing, leading to fragility fractures from minor stress. Bone takes longer to rebuild than muscle.Watch for new vertebral pain: could be a fracture.

Atrophy & sarcopenia

Unused muscles shrink. The more developed the muscle, the faster it atrophies. Sarcopenia (lean muscle loss) hits the legs first, so clients report weak legs after a few days in bed.Prevent: early mobility, active ROM, and enough protein.

Joint contractures

Joint stuck in a bent, nonfunctional position. May need surgery to fix.Prevent: move every joint at least every 8 hr; check alignment every 2 hr.

Foot drop

Can't dorsiflex (pull toes up). Toes point down and drag when walking. Caused by nerve entrapment and a shortened calf and Achilles tendon.Assess gait for toe dragging; splints as prescribed.

Memory hook · contractures

Flexors win the tug-of-war

Flexor muscles are naturally stronger than extensors. When both atrophy, the flexors pull the joint into a bent position and the collagen stiffens it there.

Cardiovascular

Orthostatic hypotension

BP drops and client gets dizzy when sitting or standing up. Raises fall risk.Change positions slowly; check lying, sitting, and standing vitals.

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT)

Clot in a deep vein. Thick blood + weak calf muscles = venous stasis. Worst complication: pulmonary embolism.Look for unilateral edema and check pulses and cap refill. Prevent with antiembolism stockings, SCDs, lower-leg exercises (ankle flexing, foot circles), fluids, frequent ambulation, and anticoagulants as prescribed.

Cardiac deconditioning

Fluid shifts cause diuresis, lower blood volume, and less work for the heart, so the heart muscle atrophies.Increase activity gradually and watch HR and BP during activity.

Memory hook · orthostatic hypotension

"20/10 in 3"

Systolic drop of 20 mm Hg or more, OR diastolic drop of 10 or more, within 3 minutes of sitting or standing up.

Slides content Promoting venous return

Goals: get blood from the legs back to the heart, prevent DVT, and prevent PE. Methods: elastic antiembolism stockings ("TED hose"), SCDs, repositioning, and active or passive ROM.

Respiratory

Lying supine limits chest expansion, pushes abdominal organs up into the diaphragm, and makes coughing weak. Dehydration thickens mucus.

Atelectasis

Partial or complete lung collapse from shallow breathing.Incentive spirometer, deep breathing and coughing, HOB 30–45°, turn every 2 hr.

Pneumonia

Thick secretions pool and trap pathogens.Fluids to thin secretions, HOB 30–45°, turn every 2 hr, prone if appropriate.

Pressure damage, least to worst

  1. Skin intact: persistent redness, temperature difference, firmness
  2. Into the skin: lighter area, blister, or shallow pink/red wound
  3. Beyond the skin: open wound, fat or granulation tissue visible
  4. Deep: exposed muscle, ligament, or bone, dead tissue

Psychological

Loss of independence, privacy, work, and hobbies can cause frustration, anxiety, depression, and social isolation.

Monitor emotional status every shift, encourage them to express feelings, encourage visits and interaction.

Quick check

A client on bed rest can't pull their toes up toward their knee and drags their toes while walking. What is this?

Foot drop. It's an inability to dorsiflex, not plantar flex.

BP lying 128/80, standing 104/74 two minutes later. Orthostatic?

Yes. Systolic dropped 24 mm Hg (20 or more) within 3 minutes.

Which intervention prevents atelectasis: incentive spirometer or high-fiber diet?

Incentive spirometer. It promotes slow, deep breaths that expand the lungs.

Promoting mobility

Ambulation builds strength, prevents complications, and shortens hospital stays.

Before ambulating

Do the mobility assessment. Have the client sit on the side of the bed a few minutes first (orthostatic hypotension). Nonskid footwear, clear path, tubes and IV lines positioned.

Assistive devices

Gait belt

Over clothing at the waist. Snug, with room to slip your fingers under. Shift it for abdominal incisions or drains.

Cane

Top at the wrist crease with arms at sides; elbow bent 20–30° when gripping. Quad cane = broader base for more support. On stairs, keep the cane level with the weaker leg.

Walker

Top at the wrist; elbow bent 15°. Move the walker, then step with the weaker leg, then the stronger. Standard (no wheels) gives the most support but needs strength. Never on stairs or escalators.

Crutches

Usually younger clients with upper body strength. Pads 1–2 inches below the axilla, grips at hip level, elbows bent 20–30°. Weight goes on the hands, not the armpits, to avoid crutch palsy (numbness and tingling).

Known mnemonic · canes and crutches on stairs

Up with the good, down with the bad

Going up, step up with the stronger leg first, then bring up the cane or crutches with the weaker leg. Going down, lead with the cane or crutches and the weaker leg.

Memory hook · elbow angles

The walker is the odd one out

Walker = 15°. Cane and crutches = 20–30°. Everything else (top at the wrist) matches.

Range of motion

Active ROM

Client moves the joint on their own.

Passive ROM

Someone else moves the relaxed joint. Keeps the joint flexible but does not prevent muscle loss or bone demineralization (no contraction, no weight-bearing).

Known mnemonic · ab vs add

Abduction abducts (takes away) the limb from the midline. Adduction adds it back.

Exercise

Adults: at least 150 min/week of moderate aerobic activity (30 min, 5 days). Older adults benefit from programs that mix aerobics, strength, and balance (adapted yoga, resistance bands). After illness, start with a few minutes and build up.

Heat & cold therapy Slides content

Heat

Increases circulation and tissue metabolism, relaxes muscles, eases stiffness and pain. Moist: warm compress, soaks, sitz bath. Dry: hot pack, heating pad, warming blanket.

Heat cautions

Bony prominences are more sensitive. No heat over metal devices, under an immobile client, or on a pregnant client's abdomen. Never in the first 24 hr of a traumatic injury, or with active bleeding, noninflammatory edema, or some skin disorders.

Cold

Decreases inflammation, swelling, bleeding, fever, muscle spasms, and pain. Moist: cold compress, soaks. Dry: ice bag, collar, or glove, cold pack, cooling blanket.

Cold cautions

Don't use with cold intolerance, vascular insufficiency, open wounds, or disorders cold makes worse.

Memory hook

Heat opens up, cold shuts down

Heat brings blood in and loosens things up. Cold clamps down on swelling, bleeding, and pain, which is why a fresh injury gets cold, not heat.

Older adults

Stooped posture (kyphosis, most common in older females), poor balance and reflexes, slow and stiff joints with ankle and foot weakness, less muscle mass and endurance, and lower vision and depth perception. Result: slower, unsteadier walking and more falls. Building muscle strength helps preserve mobility.

Quick check

A client can stand from a chair using a cane. What equipment do you use to ambulate them?

Gait belt. That's Level 3, minimal assist.

Client using a walker: which moves first after the walker, the weaker or stronger leg?

The weaker leg, then the stronger.

Why doesn't passive ROM prevent muscle atrophy?

The muscle isn't contracting and the bone isn't bearing weight. It only preserves joint flexibility.

Hygiene & basic care

Let the client do as much as they can. Hygiene time is assessment time.

The body's barriers

Skin

Largest organ and first line of defense: a physical barrier plus Langerhans cells, which activate the immune response.

Mucous membranes

Line the respiratory, digestive, and urinary tracts. Mucus traps pathogens; nasal cilia push particles out to be coughed or sneezed.

Teeth

Chew food into a swallowable ball, support and shape the face, help with speech. Mouth bacteria make acid that dissolves enamel, leading to caries, infection, and tooth loss.

Nails

Layers of keratin that protect fingertips and toes. The cuticle and hyponychium block infection underneath.

Tooth layers, outside in

Enamel (hardest material in the body)→Dentin (shields the pulp)→Pulp (vessels and nerves)

Hand hygiene

Prevents health care–associated infections (HAIs), the most common adverse events in client care.

Alcohol-based sanitizer (60% alcohol)

Default choice: before touching a client, before sterile technique, dirty-to-clean site on the same client, after touching the client or their environment, after body fluids, right after removing gloves. Rub about 20 sec until dry.

Soap and water

15–20 sec of vigorous rubbing. Avoid hot water (dries skin). Dry with a disposable towel and use it to turn off the faucet.

Memory hook · when sanitizer won't cut it

Soap for the 3 S's

Soiled (visibly dirty hands) · Stool (infectious diarrhea) · Spores (C. difficile)

Skin integrity

Incontinence is a major cause of breakdown. Hips, genitals, buttocks, and perineum are hit hardest. Clean with a pH-balanced cleanser. Mild soap has fewer irritants.

Development & culture

Newborns & infants

Bathe 3 times a week the first year (more dries skin). Sponge baths until the cord falls off (1–2 weeks). One or two drops of blood when it falls = expected; active bleeding = call the provider. Cord infection: foul yellow discharge, red skin, crying when touched.

Diaper dermatitis

Most common at 9–12 months. Change diapers often, clean with water and a soap-free cleanser, apply emollients as a barrier.

Teens

Bathe daily, wash hair at least every other day, daily deodorant, noncomedogenic cleansers.

Older adults

May resist bathing (pain, fear of falling, water, or temperature, low energy). Find the reason and offer choices. Ignoring a refusal to bathe can be considered abuse.

Privacy & culture

Ask before exposing the body and expose only the part you need, briefly. Many clients prefer a same-gender nurse. Respect faith-based routines.

Clients with disabilities

Stroke

Hemiparesis = weakness on one side. Hemiplegia = paralysis on one side. Affects the side opposite the stroke. Poor oral care → aspiration pneumonia. Shower instead of tub (fewer falls) with a shower chair or tub bench, long-handled brush, handheld shower head.

Spinal cord injury

May not feel hot or cold, so check the water temperature to prevent burns. Wet skin is fragile: dry carefully with a soft towel, especially the groin and buttocks.

Visual impairment

Can learn to shave: feel the face first, practice with an empty razor or with the electric razor off, use the free hand to guide and pull skin taut. Teach oral care with large print, Braille, audio, and dental models.

Bariatric

Deep skin folds (abdomen, groin, buttocks, under breasts, behind knees, elbows, ankles, neck) trap sweat and cause rashes and infection. Wash with pH-balanced liquid soap and dab dry. Barrier creams for incontinence.

Dementia

Loses ADL ability over time. Try a towel bath (warm towel with no-rinse soap), bathing on the commode, sitting in a recliner, or one body part per day. Offer choices, schedule toileting, limit caffeine and fluids late in the day. Poor oral care → aspiration pneumonia.

Amputation

Battery-powered toothbrush, grab bars, bath seat, anti-slip mats and bath strips, supplies within reach. Wash the residual limb daily with soapy water, rinse, dry thoroughly, report skin changes.

Memory hook · dressing after a stroke

Out with the unaffected, in with the affected

Undressing: clothes come off the unaffected side first. Dressing: clothes go on the affected side first.

Bathing & linens

Shower beats a bed bath; bed bath is the last resort.

Slides content Warm bath water is 43–46°C (110–115°F).

Linen changes

Ask about PRN pain medication before an occupied bed change. Wear gloves, keep soiled linen away from your body, and put it straight into the designated receptacle.

Head to toe

Hair

Brush from the ends up toward the roots, holding the hair at the scalp. Ask about products. Cowashing (conditioner only) suits dry, curly, or coarse hair. Locs aren't combed. Check for scalp pressure injuries under protective styles.

Lice & dandruff

Lice: notify the provider immediately. Dandruff: itchy, flaky scalp.

Eyes

Wash from the inner to outer canthus, a clean part of the cloth for each eye. Poor eyesight raises fall risk. Keep glasses clean and document vision aids.

Ears

Washcloth over your finger. No cotton swabs. Drainage is abnormal and may mean infection.

Feet

Wash daily and dry between the toes. Lotion on the feet, not between the toes. For diabetes or poor circulation: warm (not hot) water, don't soak (dries skin), inspect for cuts and blisters. Trim straight across and file, per facility policy.

Slides content Your slides say podiatry should be the only specialty to clip toenails for clients with diabetes, poor circulation, or immunosuppression; an emery board can file long toenails down. The module says to check facility policy and, if trimming is allowed, cut straight across and file.

Fingernails

Soak 10 min, clean under nails with an orange stick, cut straight across, file, apply lotion. Longer nails hide more germs.

Known mnemonic · washing the face

Clean to dirty

Eyes first, then forehead, cheeks, nose, ears, neck.

Slides content Shaving Flagged

Clients who bleed easily, take anticoagulants, or have a low platelet count should use an electric razor. Shave in the direction of hair growth. Use extra caution with nail care for clients on anticoagulants, too.

Perineal care

Wear clean gloves. Let the client do it if appropriate.

Vagina

Wash front to back to lower UTI risk.

Uncircumcised penis Flagged

Retract the foreskin and clean underneath, then always return the foreskin to prevent swelling, pain, and poor circulation to the tip.

Catheter care

Daily. Genitals → meatus → catheter, moving away from the body. Hold the catheter near the meatus so you don't tug it.

Supplies: washcloths, skin cleanser, skin protectant cream, waterproof pad. Not cotton swabs, peroxide, gauze, talc, sterile gloves, or povidone-iodine.

Oral care

Daily routine

Brush twice a day for 2 min with fluoride toothpaste and a soft brush (battery-powered is best). Clean the tongue. Floss daily. Fluoride mouthwash.

Healthy vs not

Healthy: pink, moist lips and tongue, clean intact teeth, visible saliva. Unhealthy: dry mouth, chipped teeth, coated tongue, plaque, thrush (may mean dentures with poor hygiene).

Why it matters

Plaque → gingivitis → periodontitis. Mouth bacteria can reach the heart. Ventilated clients need nurse-provided oral care to prevent ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP).

Dentures

Clean them to remove trapped food and prevent thrush. Lost dentures affect eating, speech, mental health, and wallets.

Slides content Oral care for an unconscious client

Suction ready at the bedside, client on their side when able, foam swabs or an oral suction kit, and never put your fingers in their mouth. Assess swallowing and aspiration risk first.

Quick check

A nurse finishes caring for a client with C. diff. Sanitizer or soap and water?

Soap and water. Spores need soap.

Client with left-sided hemiplegia needs a shirt on. Which arm goes in first?

The left (affected) arm.

Which is a risk of bathing a client with a basin?

Contamination with pathogens. Basins can carry hospital pathogens.

Client education

Ongoing, goal-driven, and interactive. Required by the Joint Commission.

The PN's role

Reinforce teaching the RN has already done.

Why teach

Builds skills and accountability for self-care, supports lifestyle changes, helps clients advocate for themselves and manage their care.

Documentation

Verifies care, supports continuity, and affects reimbursement.

Knowles's principles of adult learning

Relevance

Knows why it applies to them.A client wants to learn foot care after hearing it can prevent an amputation.

Self-directed

Controls choices and owns the consequences.A client chooses when to practice their insulin injections.

Life experience

Builds on what they already know.A client who cooks relates carb counting to following a recipe.

Readiness

Ready, willing, and able right now.A client in hypertensive crisis isn't ready to learn new meds.

Task-centered

Tied to real problems, not memorization.Practicing on their own glucometer instead of memorizing ranges.

Motivation

Internal and external both needed; internal is what makes it stick.A client quits smoking so they can keep up with their grandkids.

Health literacy

Ability to obtain, read, and understand health info. Low literacy → more med errors, ED visits, admissions, and deaths. Universal precautions (AHRQ): treat every client as if they might have low health literacy. Plain language: "Your blood pressure is a little high" instead of "You have hypertension."

Low-stimulus environment

Good ventilation and lighting, comfortable temperature, low noise, privacy. TV off, and don't teach at mealtimes.

Repetition & feedback

Repeat to build confidence. Give feedback during and after, like watching a client take their own BP.

Barriers to learning

Pain & discomfort

Assess and treat comfort before teaching.

Language

Hospital-based or telephone interpreter. Never family or friends (confidentiality, accuracy).

Hearing

Face to face, speak clearly, cut background noise, hands away from your face, write key info down.

Vision

Large print, voice-generated computers, Braille.

Cognition

Involve family; evaluate often.

Psychomotor

Fine motor: small muscles (writing, opening med packages, injections, dressing changes). Gross motor: large movements (walking, ROM, propelling a wheelchair).

Teaching strategies

Group

Two or more clients. Childbirth classes, support groups, diabetes clinics.

Individual

One-on-one: at the bedside or a clinic visit.

Teach-back

Client explains in their own words or demonstrates the skill. Shows what they understood and what needs reteaching.

Digital

Websites, multimedia, mobile devices. Consistent, and works best combined with traditional teaching.

The teaching plan

Mirrors the nursing process, but focused on learning needs.

  1. Assessment: learning needs, readiness, motivation, resources, health literacy, disabilities, preferences, culture
  2. Analysis (RN only): identify and prioritize learning needs
  3. Planning: individualized plan with goals and strategies
  4. Implementation: remind them why it matters, encourage questions; mix verbal, demonstration, print, video, teach-back
  5. Evaluation: were goals met? Teach-back or return demonstration. If not, is the goal realistic or do the methods need to change?
