How to connect the dots

The thinking behind questions that mix topics.

Ask "what does this do to the body?"

Most mixed questions take a fact you learned in one setting and drop it into another. If you know what something does, not just where you learned it, you can follow it anywhere. Run every new situation through the clinical judgment model Week 2: what are the cues, what do they connect to, what's most dangerous, what do I do?

You learned

Epinephrine drives fight or flight: heart rate, BP (blood pressure) and cardiac output go up Week 5.

Connect it

So when the heart stops in cardiac arrest, epinephrine is given during CPR (cardiopulmonary resuscitation) to stimulate the heart and tighten blood vessels, pushing blood to the heart and brain. Same hormone, new situation.

Memory hook

Learn the job, not the chapter. For every hormone, drug, lab or position, ask "what does it do?" Then ask "where else would I want that, or fear that?"

Assess first, unless something is hurting the client right now

"Assess first" is the default in every unit. The exception: if something you're doing or giving is causing harm, stop it first, then assess.

Assess first

  • Fall history: fall-risk assessment Week 7
  • Before first walking a client: mobility assessment, dangle at the bedside Week 4
  • Before a tube feeding or medication: check placement Week 8
  • Suspected retention: bladder scan before a catheter Week 9
  • Before teaching: readiness, pain treated Week 4
  • Crisis: assess safety and self-harm risk Week 5
  • Before food after a seizure: gag reflex Week 7

Stop it first

  • IV (intravenous) site cool, pale, swollen: stop the infusion Week 8
  • Transfusion reaction: stop the transfusion Week 8
  • Coughing or choking during a tube feeding: stop the feeding Week 8
  • Heart rate drops during disimpaction or an enema: stop Week 9
  • Restraint cutting off circulation: remove it Week 7
  • Tenderness on palpation: stop palpating, notify Week 7
Memory hook

If it's going in and it's hurting, stop it. Fluids, blood, formula, an enema, a restraint: stop the source of harm, then assess.

Quick check

A client receiving IV fluids develops a cool, pale, swollen site. First action?

Stop the infusion. Something going in is causing harm.

A newly confused older adult: what three things do you think about before blaming dementia?

Low oxygen, fluid or electrolyte problems (dehydration, sodium), and infection such as a UTI (urinary tract infection).

Which comes first for a client who wants to learn insulin injections but is in pain 8/10?

Treat the pain. A physiological need comes before learning, and pain blocks learning.

Quick check

An ABG shows HCO3– (bicarbonate) 24 and a CMP shows bicarbonate 28. Are both normal?

Yes. ABG range 22–26, CMP range 23–30. Same chemical, different tests.

Poor wound healing and pressure injuries, and edema from fluid leaking out of the vessels.

Stress hormones & the nervous system

Fight or flight, cortisol and the vagus nerve explain findings in every unit.

Epinephrine (fight or flight)

You learned

The SAM (sympathetic-adrenal-medullary) system releases epinephrine and norepinephrine in seconds: HR (heart rate), BP, cardiac output and blood glucose go up; pupils and bronchi dilate; digestion slows Week 5.

Connect it
  • Cardiac arrest: epinephrine is given during CPR to restart and support the heart.
  • Severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis), such as during a blood transfusion Week 8: epinephrine opens the airways and raises BP.
  • Pain, fear and anxiety are on the tachycardia list Week 2 because they trigger the same response.
  • Glucose rises with stress, so stressed or ill clients with diabetes run high.

Cortisol and corticosteroids

You learned

Short-term cortisol helps; chronic cortisol suppresses immunity, slows healing and drives inflammation Week 5.

Connect it
  • Corticosteroid medications act like cortisol: they make a client a more susceptible host for infection Week 6 and block collagen, delaying wound healing Week 6.
  • An older adult on steroids may show a serious infection with only mild signs, since inflammation is dampened.
  • Long-term steroids are on the lists for low potassium and low calcium Week 8.

The vagus nerve: "bear down, slow down"

You learned

CN X (cranial nerve 10, the vagus) is both sensory and motor; with CN IX it controls swallowing and the gag reflex Week 7. In the resistance stage, the parasympathetic system calms the body down Week 5.

Connect it
  • Rectal stimulation fires the vagus: stop a disimpaction if the heart rate drops Week 9; a slowing pulse during an enema means stop Week 9.
  • Straining (Valsalva) can cause bradycardia, low BP and fainting Week 9, so constipation is a cardiac safety issue too.
  • Never press both carotids at once: heart rate and BP can drop Week 7.
  • Gag reflex = CN IX and X: check it before food after a seizure Week 7, before NG insertion Week 8, and before oral care on an unconscious client Week 4.
Memory hook

Bear down, slow down. Straining or rectal stimulation fires the vagus nerve, and the heart slows.

When the nurse asks the client to bear down or hold their breath

The Valsalva maneuver is bearing down with the breath held, like straining to have a bowel movement. It raises the pressure inside the chest and abdomen, and it stimulates the vagus nerve, so the heart rate can drop.

You learned

Straining can cause bradycardia, low BP and fainting Week 9.

Connect it

When bearing down is part of a procedure, watch for dizziness or a slow pulse. When straining would be harmful, prevent it: stool softeners and fiber for constipation, and splinting the incision when coughing after abdominal surgery Week 6. Straining also raises pressure in the head, which is one reason enemas are avoided with increased intracranial pressure Week 9.

Stress, mood and the gut, lungs and sleep

  • Stress speeds peristalsis (diarrhea, IBS [irritable bowel syndrome] flares); depression slows it (constipation) Week 9.
  • Anxiety or panic → hyperventilation → blows off CO2 (carbon dioxide) → respiratory alkalosis Week 8.
  • Stage 3 sleep repairs tissue and boosts immunity Week 5, which is why cluster care protects healing Week 6.
  • Pain and anxiety block learning Week 4 and sleep Week 5, so treat them first.

Quick check

Which hormone from the fight-or-flight response is given during CPR?

Epinephrine: it stimulates the heart and tightens vessels to push blood to the heart and brain.

A client on long-term prednisone (a corticosteroid) has a wound that won't heal. Connect it.

Corticosteroids act like chronic cortisol: they block collagen and suppress immunity, delaying healing and raising infection risk.

During a digital disimpaction the heart rate drops from 78 to 50. Why, and what do you do?

Rectal stimulation triggered the vagus nerve. Stop the procedure and check the client.

Why does the nurse ask the client to take a deep breath and hold it while pulling out an NG tube?

Holding the breath closes the airway, so the tube can't drip fluid into the lungs on the way out.

A client having a panic attack reports tingling fingers and lightheadedness. Which ABG pattern?

Respiratory alkalosis: fast breathing blows off CO2.

Quick check

A client on bed rest after surgery has diminished breath sounds at the bases and SpO2 92%. Likely cause and prevention?

Atelectasis from shallow breathing. Incentive spirometer, deep breathing and coughing, turning every 2 hours, head of bed raised.

Why does a client with anemia have a fast heart rate?

Less hemoglobin carries less oxygen, so the heart speeds up to deliver more.

Potassium, start to finish

The electrolyte that connects the gut, the kidneys, the heart and the menu.

Low (under 3.5)

  • Causes: loop and thiazide diuretics Week 9, diarrhea, vomiting, NG suction Week 8
  • Signs: muscle weakness, leg cramps, dysrhythmias; can cause ileus Week 9
  • Raises digoxin toxicity risk
  • Add foods: bananas, baked potatoes, oranges, spinach Week 8
  • IV potassium: diluted, on a pump, 10–20 mEq per hour, never IV push Week 8

High (over 5)

  • Cause: kidney failure; potassium-sparing diuretics
  • Signs: weakness, paralysis, life-threatening dysrhythmias
  • Renal diet limits potassium; avoid salt substitutes Week 8
  • Treat: sodium polystyrene sulfonate, also given as a retention enema Week 9; insulin moves potassium into cells, so check glucose Week 8
You learned

Urine output under 30 mL/hr gets reported Week 8.

Connect it

Kidneys have to be working before potassium is given. Low output means potassium can build up, so check urine output before giving it.

Quick check

A client on furosemide has leg cramps, weakness and hypoactive bowel sounds. Connect it.

Loop diuretic → low potassium → muscle weakness, cramps and slowed bowel (ileus risk).

Which enema lowers potassium?

A medicated retention enema with sodium polystyrene sulfonate.

Quick check

An older adult takes diphenhydramine to sleep and now can't urinate. Connect it.

Antihistamines are anticholinergic: "can't pee." It causes urinary retention.

A client with kidney failure is prescribed a drug the kidneys excrete. What do you check, and why?

BUN and creatinine: poor excretion lets the drug build up toward toxicity, so the dose may need to be lower.

A tube-fed client needs an extended-release tablet. What do you do?

Don't crush it. Ask for another form, such as a liquid, then give it separately with flushes, never mixed into the feeding.

A client on warfarin starts eating a large leafy green salad every day. Connect it.

Leafy greens are high in vitamin K, which weakens warfarin. Teach the client to keep vitamin K intake steady.

Name four problems an opioid can cause in four different units.

Slow breathing (vital signs), respiratory acidosis (acid-base), constipation (immobility and bowel), urinary retention (urinary).

Case 1 · Mr. Jones after hip surgery

Mr. Jones, 78, had hip surgery Week 2. He's on bed rest, takes opioids and has an indwelling catheter.

Day 1: temperature 39.0 °C, pulse 122. What do you think about?

Fever with tachycardia after surgery: infection is possible. Check the incision, lungs and urine; report it Week 2 Week 6.

Day 3: no bowel movement, hypoactive bowel sounds, distended abdomen, no flatus.

Opioids plus immobility plus surgery slow the gut: constipation or ileus. Check potassium too, since low potassium can cause ileus Week 9.

Day 3: suddenly confused, cloudy urine.

Think CAUTI. Urine from the sampling port before antibiotics; ask whether the catheter is still needed Week 9.

First time up: lying 130/80, standing 106/72, dizzy.

Orthostatic hypotension (systolic dropped 24). Sit him back down; he's a high fall risk Week 2 Week 7.

His left calf is swollen; the right is not.

One-sided swelling: possible DVT. Report it Week 7 Week 4.
